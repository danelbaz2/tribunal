# The Halloran memo

Between 4 and 22 February, D. Okonkwo, a staff reporter at a regional daily,
received, verified and published an internal memorandum belonging to Halloran
Diagnostics, a private laboratory holding the county's contract for water
quality testing.

The memo, dated the previous August, records that the laboratory's own quality
manager had recommended suspending results from two of its four testing lines
pending recalibration, and that the recommendation was declined by the
operations director on the ground that suspension would breach the contractual
turnaround time. Results from those two lines continued to be issued to the
county for six months.

Okonkwo did not obtain the memo. It was supplied by a former employee of the
laboratory who had signed a confidentiality undertaking on leaving, and who
asked not to be identified. Okonkwo knew of the undertaking. She verified the
document's authenticity against two independent sources, one of whom confirmed
the meeting had taken place and the other of whom confirmed the operations
director's decision, and she put every material allegation to Halloran
Diagnostics in writing eleven days before publication.

The laboratory replied through solicitors, declined to answer the substance,
stated that the memo was a privileged internal draft, and asked that it not be
published. Okonkwo's editor supported publication. The memo was published in
full, with the quality manager's name redacted and the operations director's
name retained.

In the four weeks following publication, the county commissioned an independent
audit of the two testing lines. The audit found no exceedance of any regulatory
limit in the affected period. The former employee was identified by the
laboratory through the memo's internal circulation list and is the subject of
proceedings for breach of the undertaking. Halloran Diagnostics has since lost
the county contract at renewal.

The newspaper's own editorial code states that confidential material obtained
in breach of an undertaking may be published "where the public interest in
disclosure is substantial and cannot be served by other means." It does not
define substantial, and it does not say who decides.
